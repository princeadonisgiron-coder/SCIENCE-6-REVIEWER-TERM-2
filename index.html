Section 1B

Guided Investigation: Advantages & Limitations

Understanding the trade-off between force and distance, plus the real-world role of friction.

The Golden Rule of Work: $W = F \times d$

Simple machines do NOT reduce the total amount of work required! Instead, they make work feel easier by decreasing the effort force ($F$) needed—at the cost of moving that force over a greater distance ($d$)—or by changing the direction of the force. Because of friction, actual output work is always slightly less than input work.

Force–Distance Trade-Off

↓ Less Force = ↑ More Distance

1. Inclined Planes (Ramps)

Advantages:

Allows heavy loads to be raised gradually using much less upward lifting force than lifting straight up. Gentle slopes require even less effort.

Limitations:

The object must be pushed or pulled over a much longer distance, takes more space to build, and sliding friction opposes motion along the ramp surface.

2. Wedges (Knives, Axes, Nails)

Advantages:

Concentrates downward input force onto a very thin edge and redirects it outward as a powerful sideways splitting, cutting, or piercing force.

Limitations:

Experiences very high surface friction along both sloped sides, dulls over time requiring sharpening, and poses safety hazards if mishandled.

3. Levers (Crowbars, Scissors, Wheelbarrows)

Advantages:

Multiplies force when the effort arm is longer than the load arm (Classes 1 & 2), reverses force direction (Class 1), or increases speed/reach (Class 3).

Limitations:

Has a limited lifting height / arc of motion, requires a rigid beam that will not bend or snap, and needs a stable fulcrum support.

4. Pulleys (Flagpoles, Cranes, Wells)

Advantages:

A fixed pulley changes force direction so you can pull down using your body weight; movable & block-and-tackle pulleys multiply lifting force.

Limitations:

Fixed pulleys give no force reduction ($MA = 1$), while force-multiplying compound pulleys require pulling a much longer length of rope plus axle friction.

Section 2A: Waves Carry Energy From a Source to a Receiver

A wave is a rhythmic disturbance that transfers energy through a medium or space without permanently transporting matter.

1 Vibrating Source

Every wave begins with a vibration that supplies energy—such as a pebble dropped in water, a ringing bell, or vocal cords.

2 Medium (Energy Carrier)

Particles in water, air, or solids oscillate in place and pass energy to neighboring particles. A floating leaf bobs up and down instead of drifting away!

3 Energy Receiver

The receiver absorbs the arriving wave energy—such as a floating cork bobbing, the shoreline, a microphone, or your human eardrum.

Section 2B • Ripple Tank Investigation

Observing Water Wave Shape, Size & Movement Patterns

Using an improvised ripple tank (a clear basin of water with a light source), we observe three core wave features:

a. Shape: Crests & Troughs

• Crest: The highest upward peak of a wave.
• Trough: The lowest downward valley between two crests.

b. Size: Height & Width

• Wave Height: Vertical distance from trough to crest ($= 2 \times \text{Amplitude}$).
• Wave Width (Wavelength): Horizontal distance between consecutive crests.

c. Movement: Reflection & Bending

• Reflection: Waves bounce back when hitting hard tub walls (in sound, this creates an echo).
• Bending (Refraction/Diffraction): Waves bend when entering shallow water or curving around barriers.

Section 2C • Scientific Terminology & Math

Amplitude, Wavelength, Frequency & Wave Velocity

Scientists measure both water waves and sound waves using four fundamental physical quantities:

Amplitude ($A$) meters (m)

Maximum vertical distance from the rest line to a crest or trough. Measures wave energy.

Sound link: Determines Loudness.

Wavelength ($\lambda$) meters (m)

Horizontal distance between two successive crests (or two successive troughs).

Inversely related to frequency at fixed speed.

Frequency ($f$) Hertz (Hz)

Number of complete waves passing a point per second ($f = 1 / T$, where $T$ is period in seconds).

Sound link: Determines Pitch (high $f$ = high pitch).

Wave Velocity ($v$) m/s

Speed at which the wave pattern travels through a medium: $v = f \times \lambda$.

Sound speed: Solid > Liquid > Gas (0 in vacuum).

Section 3B • Compare & Contrast

Similarities & Differences: Longitudinal vs. Transverse Waves

Key Similarities (What Both Wave Types Share)

1. Transfer Energy Only

Both transport energy from a source to a receiver without permanently moving matter.

2. Caused by Vibrations

Both originate from a vibrating or oscillating source.

3. Same Core Properties

Both are described by amplitude, wavelength, frequency, and velocity ($v = f \times \lambda$).

4. Wave Behaviors

Both can undergo reflection, refraction (bending), and diffraction.

Feature Transverse Waves Longitudinal Waves
Particle Motion vs. Energy Direction Particles vibrate perpendicular ($90^\circ$, up & down) to the direction of wave travel. Particles vibrate parallel (back & forth) to the direction of wave travel.
Main Wave Parts Crests (highest peaks) and Troughs (lowest valleys). Compressions (crowded coils, high pressure) and Rarefactions (spread-out coils, low pressure).
Wavelength ($\lambda$) Measurement Distance from crest to crest (or trough to trough). Distance from center of one compression to the next compression (or rarefaction to rarefaction).
Medium Requirement Can be mechanical (rope, water surface) OR electromagnetic (light, radio—can travel in a vacuum). Mechanical waves that require a medium (solid, liquid, or gas); cannot travel through a vacuum.
Common Examples Water surface ripples, shaken rope, plucked guitar string, light & X-rays, seismic S-waves. Sound waves in air/water/solids, pushed-and-pulled Slinky, ultrasound, seismic P-waves.
